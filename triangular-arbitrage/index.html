Triangular Arbitrage Game

A cross rate quoted by a bank should match the cross rate implied by the two dollar rates. When it does not, you can trade $1,000,000 around the triangle (dollars, then one currency, then the other, then back to dollars) and end with more dollars than you started with, at no risk. Learn how to spot the gap, race the clock on practice boards, then see why such gaps close almost as soon as they open.

    3Why it disappears

    Arbitrage removes itself. Every trader running the profitable route buys the cheap currency and sells the expensive one, and those orders move all three prices toward parity. But how far do they move?

    2. Parity, up to costs

      With no trading costs the gap is driven all the way to zero. With bid–ask spreads, trading stops once the gap is smaller than the cost of crossing three bid–ask spreads (the edge of the no-arbitrage band), so a small gap can survive. That is why many apparent gaps on the Hard boards vanish once you use bid and ask prices.

    3. Real markets are fast

      In the real interbank market, computers watch these triangles constantly, so mispricings are tiny and last a fraction of a second. The gaps in this game are much larger than you will see in practice, so you can learn the arithmetic.

    Quotation conventions follow the course: a direct quote is the home-currency (dollar) price of one foreign unit, e.g. $1.1163 per €; an indirect quote is foreign units per dollar, e.g. ¥157.47 per $. Cross rate A/B = ($/B) ÷ ($/A), or equivalently ($/B) × (A/$). In a quote X/Y, Y is the basis currency (the one unit being priced). Rip-off principle: the bid (the smaller number) is the rate when you sell the basis currency; the ask (the larger number) is the rate when you buy it. Bid–ask spread = (ask − bid) ÷ ask.

    Amounts in the steps are rounded for display; profits are computed from the quoted rates without rounding in between. Game boards and the arbitrage simulation are illustrative teaching examples, not market data.
